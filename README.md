Gamification offers innovative opportunities for the humanities, providing more engaging approaches to subjects frequently regarded as abstract, theoretical, or challenging. Combining game elements such as interactive tasks, rewards, and narrative frameworks allows for clearer and more accessible presentation of complex concepts. This method helps explain difficult ideas while maintaining their academic rigor. As competition for attention intensifies within academic and cultural contexts, effective idea presentation becomes critical. Consequently, gamification serves as a strategy to enhance the dissemination of humanities content and increase its impact.

This challenge is not unique to public-facing projects, but is already present within academic practices themselves. As Hammarfelt Björn, Sarah de Rijcke, and Alexander D. Rushforth write, the growing gamification of research through social networking services such as ResearchGate and Impactstory illustrates how visibility and competitive mechanisms have become central to scholarly communication. While these systems intensify competition, they also demonstrate that structured incentives and feedback can empower individual researchers by increasing the reach and visibility of their work. In this sense, gamification is not an external addition to the humanities, but part of an existing move toward more strategic forms of presentation.

The same logic becomes evident in educational contexts. Research *Can Gamification Influence the Academic Performance of Students?* on multimodal Gamified Learning Environments shows that gamification improves engagement and performance by structuring content and sustaining motivation. Although learning outcomes are not the primary focus here, these findings show mechanisms directly transferable to research communication and project presentations. Gamification helps convert complex material into a coherent experience that allows the audience to navigate abstract content more easily.

Insights from marketing research by Yang, Xinyi, et al. further clarify why these mechanisms are effective. According to the Stimulus-Organism-Response (SOR) model, game elements function as stimuli that shape psychological responses and lead to specific behaviours, such as increased attention, interest, and positive attitudes. Empirical studies demonstrate that gamification influences awareness and perception across a wide range of industries. These effects are especially significant for the humanities, which regularly lack explicit strategies for shaping audience engagement, even as they operate within environments that increasingly demand them.

The absence of strategic presentation is particularly evident in public perceptions of the humanities. The WhatEvery1Says (WE1S) project reveals that the humanities occupy a marginal position in media discourse and are seldom associated with concrete outcomes or discoveries. Although the humanities are deeply embedded in daily life and regarded as relatable, they are also perceived as abstract and “objectless,” diminishing their visibility and perceived impact. In this context, the widely discussed “crisis” of the humanities appears to be more a matter of communication than of relevance.

Viewed through this lens, gamification can be understood as a connective strategy that connects academic practices, educational insights, and marketing principles. Rather than simplifying content, it provides tools that help the humanities articulate their value and compete more effectively for visibility in contemporary cultural and academic spaces.

This web interface presents an example of such gamification through a project connected to my upcoming master’s thesis, which applies social network analysis (SNA) within the narrative framework of detective fiction. Using Agatha Christie’s novels as case studies, the thesis aims to map character interactions to uncover hidden relational patterns that may elucidate plot mechanics. Building on qualitative theories of formulaic literature by John G. Cawelti and on the narrative structure of the detective fiction genre by Tzvetan Todorov, this study adopts a data-driven approach to compare British and American detective fiction from 1920 to 1940. By visualizing central figures, community dynamics, and narrative progression through network metrics, the research intends to demonstrate how SNA can reveal the underlying “architectural” principles of genre suspense and reveal. The project intends to bridge computational methods with literary criticism, potentially offering new insights into the structural nuances of detective novels.
